Reflections on personal Yoga practice:

By sheer coincidence, I started trying Yoga during the pandemic in 2020. I purchased a Keep membership and followed their Hot Yoga sessions. After sticking with it for a few years, I can now perform many asanas quite well. Personally, I feel the guidance provided by video instructors often exceeds that of many in-person training classes. I follow a simple logic: any Yoga instructor wants to put their best side, the most detailed explanations, and the most refined content into their videos. Furthermore, everyone tends to slack off a bit at work. A single in-person class usually yields a relatively low real-world return for them, so few instructors actually enter a classroom with the intention of ensuring students get their money's worth. Often, classes turn into opportunities for the teacher to show off or engage in small talk. However, in video preparation, they gain higher returns due to the principle of leverage. Even if not for money, but for the sake of reputation and long-term development, they are bound to cut the fluff and present their most professional selves. I believe the same logic applies to other training, such as learning German. High-quality, refined, and systematic online courses are not easy to prepare. In an offline class, you simply watch the students' reactions; they might be there because of the teacher's fame, or simply because it's convenient—close by, right time, right location, etc. However, online courses inevitably compete with many other high-quality offerings. If one doesn't take it seriously, it's impossible to gain the trust of followers. If you lack unique insights or concise summaries, you might only succeed through the first-mover advantage, aesthetics, filming quality, or the sheer volume of videos.

Of course, if you find a trustworthy teacher who suits you, in-person classes offer benefits that online ones cannot replace: receiving feedback and participating in interaction. In a small group setting, an observant teacher can indeed help you improve faster. However, Yoga practice is not something that yields results from a single class. Sustained, injury-free, and fearless practice is the only way to improve.

Over the past two years, I have finished most of the Keep Hot Yoga series. It seems they are no longer investing in recording new Yoga courses, ads have increased, and they've introduced a 'Flow Practice' card on top of the membership, so I stopped following their routines. I transitioned to individual channels of various Yoga instructors on platforms like Bilibili and YouTube, which has also been quite rewarding.

The problem is that there are so many videos online that I could never finish them in a lifetime. They aren't very systematic; they feel more like a fitness instructor helping me stay consistent with exercise—for example, Yoga Boho Beautiful, Yoga Marison, etc. I have also tried fitness types like Emi Wong or Pamela Reif, but they seemed somewhat superficial, focusing on form without essence. That was until I watched YogaLili, a Yoga instructor with a background in psychology from Peking University. Her shares feel substantial rather than just chasing trends or quantity. In other words, many Yoga instructor influencers produce videos to keep you practicing with them, watching more and more. YogaLili's videos are meant to help you turn off the screen and practice better on your own.

Without music or guided cues, I still struggle with how to practice because I haven't specifically studied the names of asanas or memorized sequences. My self-practice often stalls. During a brief in-person class in Mount Emei, I was interrupted by the teacher's long-winded discourse. Li Xiaozhong's philosophy is powerful, focusing on feeling how to make oneself comfortable rather than getting injured through basic asanas. Indeed, Yoga has no single standard answer; even RYT200 (Registered Yoga Teacher) is not a standardized exam with one answer. Everyone has their own way of teaching and practicing. The important thing is that I must find the practice method that suits me.

I look forward to nuanced explanations for specific movements, followed by a period of uninterrupted practice where I can feel each subtle change, provide feedback, and summarize that portion.

And so, to today. I seem to have an opportunity to teach Yoga, and I find myself in a state of pressured, high-spirited excitement. I suddenly have the motivation to close the screen and practice on my own. The sequence wasn't complete; it might have truly been 'flow' yoga, adjusting from one movement to another according to my body's needs. My brain wanted to control it, but I didn't truly know the 'correct' way. I fumbled through my own practice. Perhaps I won't be able to break through and become a Yoga teacher this time due to my hay fever, but being able to start self-practice is a huge step forward. By imagining myself becoming a teacher, I am constantly thinking about what I can share and what I want to share. This is the synthesis of my experience following videos and my own reflections:

1. The essence of Yoga or meditation is to bring you to the present moment, the present place, and the present body. In other words, focus on your breath rather than your cravings or delusions, which are like a TV constantly playing in your head. Observing your breath at every moment is not easy. Thus, we have the practice of Yoga asanas; those challenging, unstable movements force you to focus on your breath and the here and now. Because if your mind is wandering, you cannot remain stable.

2. The most important aspects of Yoga, in my opinion, are **breath, stretch, and stability**.
In the past, when following videos, I often started exhaling when I was supposed to inhale; I frequently did it backward. I recall an instructor mentioning that she wouldn't dictate when to inhale or exhale, and that we should follow our own rhythm. That is correct. We aren't here to 'learn' to breathe, but to observe the breathing we already do naturally. We don't have to force ourselves to follow external breathing cues.
However, in asana practice, there are small tips that can help inject breath into every movement. This is an insight from YogaLili's videos:
**Inhale**: The corresponding action or intention is **extension, upward**, and forward. Feel each inhalation bringing you 1 millimeter or 1 micron more of stretch.
**Exhale**: The corresponding action or intention is **stability**, relaxation, and feeling **rooted** downward. Feel each exhalation connecting us better to the ground—stable, safe, and reliable.
If you stay in one position for several breaths without an obvious upward or downward movement, you should still consciously extend from your chest and abdomen during the inhale, and root down into the ground during the exhale.

3. This leads to the question: **How do we judge if we are forcing ourselves with excessive effort, or improving through healthy effort?** What constitutes 'too much' versus facing suffering without escaping? This is a common life problem as well as a Yoga asana problem. Let's focus on Yoga first. Usually, stretching (flexibility/mobility) or stability (muscle strength/core strength) for any body part has many variations. Different people at different times are suited for different degrees of stretching or exercise. It's like the weight in weightlifting, the height in high jump, or the angle of a freestyle rotation. This judgment can only be based on our own knowledge and understanding of our body and soul. No one else, not even a coach, can decide this for you. Just like Eileen Gu decides what action to try next based on her self-understanding.
Yogalili proposed a way to judge: <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8GptZRQUTwE&t=15s> —that is, can you maintain smooth breathing in an asana, and can you inject your breath into that moment, rather than holding your breath? If you can breathe smoothly but feel a bit tired, you can stay in that static pose for a few breaths. If you cannot breathe smoothly, return to a variation where you can.

4. This brings us to the many variations available for stretching every part of the body. This is content I really want to summarize for my own practice or future teaching.
I once followed a flow yoga class on Keep that I found very interesting. It started from a lying position, feeling the 'cat bow' (supine cat pose) and 'cow bow' (supine cow pose). By the way, B.K.S. Iyengar mentions in 'Light on Pranayama' that the hardest yoga asana is not wheel pose, shoulder stand, headstand, or handstand, but Savasana (corpse pose). I found this translation quite strange at first—why use 'corpse'? In essence, it is to experience the boundary between life and death. The difficulty of this pose is like the most difficult part for an actor being the portrayal of psychological depth. The external form is easy to imitate; many other asanas might look like the 'real thing' even if we haven't integrated our body and mind or injected breath into them. But Savasana truly tests our understanding of Yoga. To be awake without falling asleep, slowly withdrawing the senses, gradually slowing the heartbeat, until one enters a state of 'no-self'.
