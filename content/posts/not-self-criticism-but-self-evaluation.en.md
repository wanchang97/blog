I seem to be afraid to face my own actions. I have no problem taking action, but I seem afraid to take responsibility for the outcome.

## 1. The Habit of Being Afraid to Look

Take group photos, for example. Although I love beauty and really hope to have nice photos, I might cooperate during the shoot, but among the many people scrambling to look at the group photo, I am the one who dislikes myself in it the most.

Likewise, I am afraid to look in the rearview mirror. When I was learning to drive in China, the instructor kept telling me to check the rearview mirror, look over my shoulder, etc. I treated these as mere orders, pretending to look in the mirror without actually paying attention—I was afraid that if I really saw a car closing in on me, I wouldn't know what to do. I felt that rather than letting myself be disappointed, it was better to leave it as an unresolved mystery (much like that fake glance at the mirror). Or perhaps, in most cases, I won't look good in group photos. I feel inferior and feel I cannot bear the consequences, so it is better to pretend I don't care.

That is why I am so afraid to examine my own photos, examine my own writing, examine my language, examine my decisions, examine my videos, or examine and expose my past.

## 2. Never Estimating My Own Score

From childhood through all my exams (including the Gaokao), I have never once estimated my score after finishing. It’s not that I don’t care; it’s that I care too much, while simultaneously being very pessimistic—feeling that even if I did estimate my score, there might be an even worse outcome (for instance, the grader being in a bad mood), so it is better to just know the final score and get it over with. Therefore, I have never thought about appealing a grade or result; my attitude has mostly been one of resignation and leaving it to fate. It is like the ox described in Wang Xiaobo's "The Golden Age" that is locked up, knowing only to eat grass and work, knowing nothing else, to the point that it doesn't even need to be tied up when being slaughtered.

## 3. Loss of Self-Evaluation Training

In the past, I might have been praised by teachers for this "seemingly forward-looking" habit of endurance, or sometimes I would forcibly erase my memories after an exam, only to be surprised when I performed well. But I feel that this has actually led to the loss of self-evaluation training and the mastery of my own subjectivity.

I am accustomed to letting the outside world—some anonymous evaluator—judge whether my past was excellent or not, whether it was worthwhile or not, while never valuing my own voice and feelings.

How do I evaluate my past performance? Was there any preparation process that made me feel particularly amazed by myself? Was there a moment when I felt my nerves suddenly fully connected and activated? Did I enjoy the process? In this exam or these various exhibitions, did I gain any experiences that I would never have had if I hadn't studied them? What were the real, objective limitations beyond my personal control? What are the areas where I know I was creative and resilient?

## 4. The Illusion of External Evaluation

However, in real life, whether it is projects, various design presentations, your life arrangements, or your personal choices, there is actually no such thing as a truly objective evaluation. At this point, the habit of seeking recognition or a grade from the outside becomes quite ridiculous. Because that is merely another person's subjective judgment—and one coming from someone who doesn't understand you at all. It is a bit like putting your head on a guillotine and letting passersby chop it off based on their mood.
