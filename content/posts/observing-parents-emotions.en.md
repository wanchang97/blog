Dad likes to focus on the future; he is concerned with things like Musk, Starlink, commemorative notes, AI, emerging trends, and making money. This is the only form of success recognized by his values.

He demands this from those around him, and he demands it of himself.

Therefore, when those around him fail to meet these expectations, or when he fails himself, his frustration turns into rage.

He wants to control things so they go exactly as he expects. But when he encounters things he cannot understand or control, he becomes angry. Getting angry is a manifestation of having no other choices. If we had other ways to handle embarrassment, we could simply use self-deprecation or encouragement to face it calmly. As long as there are other ways to solve a problem, it shouldn't trigger such intense emotions. So, his outbursts are actually a sign of vulnerability—a signal that he needs help.

Coincidentally, I have also observed this in my father-in-law. When small things go wrong—for example, packing leftovers and spilling a little on the counter or the floor—he gets angry and starts blaming those around him, like my mother-in-law. In these middle-aged and elderly men, I see a common thread. On the surface, it looks like the unconscious suppression, belittling, or even intimidation of women within a patriarchal society. But in reality, it may be a more universal human experience: the inability to accept a self that is "not always powerful" and in control of everything. Their inner selves are very fragile, so they want to shift the blame, cover it up, and hide the side of themselves that is out of control. The image that comes to my mind is of Shi Tiesheng in his early 20s, smashing plates at home after suddenly becoming paralyzed. It is the image of a "person" who has lost their mobility, the weakness and anger of an old man who cannot stand up after falling down with his cane. They project and shift their emotions onto the people around them, but deep down, they are perhaps not blaming those people, but rather trying to overcome their own incompetence and regain a sense of control over their lives.

At such moments, when Dad suddenly gets angry and begins to blame her, Mom often becomes fearful and anxious, becoming even more cautious. She expresses her helplessness through fear. However, for Dad, what he cares about is whether the problem or difficulty is solved, and whether he can still control his life—not whether the people around him fear him. In other words, nobody actually wants to be feared by others. Therefore, fear does not solve any problems, nor does it alleviate or improve any relationships. My advice is for Mom to prioritize protecting herself first, not to be afraid, and to give Dad quiet space and time. Mom should continue doing what she needs to do, allowing Dad to digest his own emotions and figure out his own problems and hang-ups. Mom often says she doesn't want to provoke Dad, and she doesn't know why she is frequently blamed. My first point is that Mom should not be responsible for Dad's anger. No one can be responsible for another person's emotions. After all, as humans, in a life filled with vanity and fame, we all get confused, doubt ourselves, and hope for more, better, and longer-lasting things. Yet, we all eventually have to face the day when we are powerless. Dad needs to reconcile with his own desires. Mom needs to reconcile with the reality that she doesn't have the ability to "enlighten" Dad. In the end, we all just need to acknowledge the parts of ourselves that we cannot control and learn to live with our own emotions.
